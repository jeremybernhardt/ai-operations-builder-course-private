## Scenario and user

An AI builder has proposed a small change to a synthetic budget-variance report. The finance manager needs every department-and-account row preserved, correct dollar and percentage calculations, correct exception logic, and explicit handling when the approved budget is zero. The proposed output looks plausible and the builder reports passing tests, but implementation acceptance requires an operator to compare the builder's plain-language change claims with the approved plan and request evidence aimed at the material risks.

This is a static change-review lesson. The fixture contains an AI-generated change summary and a few already-translated implementation excerpts. It is not executable project code and is not part of the Expense File Readiness Checker or the future finance flagship.

## Why this matters

`M0.5-L1` established what should be built. `M0.5-L2` established how to approve a plan before work begins. This lesson adds the next control: compare the behavior claimed for a proposed implementation with the approved plan and decide what evidence is needed before accepting it.

A diff can prove that specific text changed between two snapshots. It cannot by itself prove that the change satisfies the requirement, that unchanged surrounding logic is safe, that the tests are independent, that the test cases cover the important boundaries, or that the displayed output came from the reviewed code and correct source data.

At this point in the course, Python has not been taught. An AI-enabled financial analyst would normally ask AI to summarize a proposed diff in business terms, then verify the high-risk claims using requirements, known-answer outputs, and a few targeted implementation facts. Jeremy is not expected to translate raw Python independently in this lesson. Direct raw-code review becomes a larger part of the work after the SQL and Python foundations.

This capability recurs when Jeremy reviews AI-generated SQL, Python/pandas, dashboards, automations, and agent tools later in the course.

## Learning objectives

By the checkpoint, Jeremy will be able to:

1. explain what a diff and an AI-generated change summary prove and do not prove;
2. evaluate the AI's plain-language description of changed behavior without relying on independent Python interpretation;
3. map each claimed behavior to an approved requirement or label it unrelated or unclear;
4. identify a material risk that plausible output or builder-authored tests could conceal;
5. pair deterministic output evidence with targeted inspection of the relevant logic; and
6. decide whether a change is ready, needs revision, or must be rejected.

## Terms for this lesson

- **Diff:** a line-oriented comparison between two snapshots of text.
- **Context line:** an unchanged line shown to locate a change.
- **Change summary:** an AI-generated plain-language description of the behavior added, removed, or altered by a diff.
- **Behavior traceability:** a plain-language link from a changed behavior to the requirement it is meant to serve.
- **Targeted inspection:** reviewing the small section of logic where a material rule is implemented, such as a denominator, filter, join, threshold, null rule, or return value.
- **Deterministic evidence:** a check with fixed inputs and an objectively expected result, such as a known-answer calculation or row-count reconciliation.
- **Circular verification:** relying only on tests or examples created from the same possibly mistaken assumption as the implementation.
- **Regression:** an unintended failure in behavior that previously worked.

## Teaching method

### Teach

Explain where a diff and AI-generated change summary fit between plan approval and implementation acceptance. Teach the review sequence: behavior traceability, scope classification, material-risk analysis, paired output-and-logic evidence, and a bounded readiness decision. Demonstrate the complete method on an adjacent invoice-aging example using business rules and already-translated implementation facts before showing the practice fixture.

### Practice

Use the budget-variance change packet as one coherent implementation-approval review. Jeremy audits the AI's summary against the approved change, distinguishes intended from unrelated or unclear behavior, identifies at least one concealed material risk, requests deterministic evidence and targeted inspection, and decides whether the change is ready, needs revision, or must be rejected. The instructor or AI supplies the plain-language meaning of any implementation excerpt.

### Demonstrate

After the practice review is verified, present a genuinely different static change packet with reduced scaffolding. The transfer fixture and answer-specific conclusions remain withheld until Jeremy has made a genuine practice attempt. Raw Python interpretation remains outside the assessment.

## Learner deliverable

An annotated diff review containing:

1. an audit of the AI-supplied plain-language inventory of changed behaviors;
2. an `intended`, `unrelated`, or `unclear` classification for each material behavior with a link to the approved change where applicable;
3. at least one material risk that the sample output or builder-authored tests could conceal;
4. deterministic verification requests with exact known answers where the code performs a real calculation or boundary decision;
5. targeted logic-inspection requests tied to the risks, with AI translation where needed, rather than a request to read the whole file;
6. a `ready`, `needs revision`, or `reject` decision with reasoning; and
7. one remaining limitation of the review evidence.

AI may organize the final artifact after Jeremy makes or approves the substantive judgments. A polished AI-written review alone is not mastery evidence.

## Review sequence

1. Read the approved behavior and constraints before reading the AI's change summary.
2. Compare each claimed behavior with the approved requirement it is supposed to serve.
3. Classify each behavior as intended, unrelated, or unclear.
4. Identify where plausible output could hide a calculation, threshold, population, row-preservation, null, or source-lineage error.
5. Request an exact known-answer check or reconciliation for each material risk.
6. Ask AI to expose and translate only the implementation facts that control those rules, then compare them with the approved definition.
7. Decide whether the reviewed change is ready, needs a bounded revision, or violates the approved boundary strongly enough to reject.

## Practice review rubric

The practice artifact is ready for the transfer check only when:

- every material changed behavior is available in plain language and audited against the approved plan;
- approved and unapproved behavior are not conflated;
- at least one hidden logic or completeness risk is identified;
- builder-authored tests and one plausible sample do not serve as the only evidence;
- exact fixtures are requested for real calculations and decision boundaries;
- targeted inspection names the relevant rule and requests AI explanation when syntax has not yet been taught, rather than demanding whole-codebase reading; and
- the readiness decision follows from the identified requirements, risks, and missing or passing evidence.
